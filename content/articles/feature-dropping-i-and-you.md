Translate "I am a student" word by word and you get {私|わたし}は{学生|がくせい}だ. That is correct. But in real life, most people would just say {学生|がくせい}だ.

## Context does the work

In Japanese, if the listener can tell who or what you mean, you leave it out. That covers "I", "you", "it" and "they".

::: example
ja: {学生|がくせい} だ。
en: (I'm) a student.
:::

::: example
ja: おいしい！
en: (This is) delicious!
:::

::: dialogue
A: {昨日|きのう} {何|なに} を {食|た}べた？ // What did (you) eat yesterday?
B: カレー を {食|た}べた。 // (I) ate curry.
:::

Nobody said "you" or "I", but the meaning is clear. That is why this paper's translations put the missing words in brackets.

## Saying {私|わたし} too often

Starting every sentence with {私|わたし}は sounds stiff, and even a little self-centred. Use it when you are introducing yourself, or when you want to contrast yourself with someone else.

::: example
ja: {私|わたし} は {学生|がくせい} だ。{兄|あに} は {先生|せんせい} だ。
en: I'm a student. My brother is a teacher.
note: Here {私|わたし}は makes a contrast, so it is natural.
:::

## And "you"?

The word あなた exists, but people rarely use it to someone's face. It can sound distant. Use the person's name with さん instead.

::: example
ja: {田中|たなか}さん は {行|い}く？
en: Are you going, Tanaka?
note: Talking to Tanaka, using their name instead of "you".
:::
