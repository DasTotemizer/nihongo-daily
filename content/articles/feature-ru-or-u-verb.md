Japanese verbs come in two big groups, and every form you learn depends on which group a verb is in. Here is how to tell.

## Step one: does it end in る?

If the verb does not end in る, it is an う-verb. {飲|の}む, {話|はな}す, {書|か}く and {買|か}う are all う-verbs. Done.

## Step two: what comes before る?

If it ends in る, look at the sound just before it.

- If it is an *e* or *i* sound, it is **usually** a る-verb: {食|た}べる (ta-**be**-ru), {見|み}る (**mi**-ru), {起|お}きる (o-**ki**-ru).
- If it is an *a*, *o* or *u* sound, it is always an う-verb: {分|わ}かる (wa-**ka**-ru), {乗|の}る (**no**-ru).

## The sneaky ones

Some verbs look like る-verbs but are really う-verbs. These are worth learning by heart.

::: vocab
- {帰|かえ}る | go home | う-verb
- {入|はい}る | enter | う-verb
- {走|はし}る | run | う-verb
- {知|し}る | know | う-verb
- {切|き}る | cut | う-verb
:::

The negative shows the difference.

::: example
ja: {帰|かえ}る → {帰|かえ}らない
en: go home → don't go home
note: An う-verb, so る becomes ら.
:::

::: example
ja: {見|み}る → {見|み}ない
en: see → don't see
note: A る-verb, so る simply drops.
:::

::: callout
title: Listen for the ます form
If you hear a verb in its polite form, the group is obvious: {帰|かえ}ります shows the り of an う-verb, while {食|た}べます has no り.
:::
