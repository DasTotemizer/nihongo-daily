Spoken Japanese has casual だ and polite です. Formal writing has a third voice: **である**. It is neither friendly nor polite, just neutral and authoritative.

## である replaces だ

::: vocab
- {学生|がくせい}だ → {学生|がくせい}である | is a student | statement
- {学生|がくせい}じゃない → {学生|がくせい}ではない | is not a student | negative
- {学生|がくせい}だった → {学生|がくせい}であった | was a student | past
- {学生|がくせい}じゃなかった → {学生|がくせい}ではなかった | was not a student | past negative
:::

::: example
ja: {吾輩|わがはい} は {猫|ねこ} で ある。
en: I am a cat. (the opening of a famous novel)
:::

::: breakdown
ja: {日本|にほん} は {島国|しまぐに} で あり、{四|よっ}つ の {大|おお}きな {島|しま} から {成|な}る。
en: Japan is an island nation, made up of four large islands.
- {島国|しまぐに}であり = is an island nation, and
- {四|よっ}つの{大|おお}きな{島|しま} = four large islands
- から{成|な}る = is made up of
:::

## であり: the formal て form

In writing, sentences are often joined with the ます stem instead of the て form. For である, that gives であり.

::: example
ja: {彼|かれ} は {医者|いしゃ} で あり、{作家|さっか} でも ある。
en: He is a doctor, and also a writer.
:::

## ではない in writing

In writing, じゃない becomes ではない, and the ず negative appears often: {知|し}られず, without being known.

::: callout
title: Where you will see it
News articles, academic papers, Wikipedia, essays and legal text. Personal emails and blogs usually stay with です and ます.
:::
