The 46 basic hiragana cover most of Japanese, but not all of it. How do you write *ga*, *kyo* or the double *t* in *kitte* (stamp)? Japanese solves this without adding new letters. It adds small marks to the ones you already know.

## Two dashes and a circle

Add two small dashes, called *dakuten*, to the top right of a character and its consonant becomes voiced: your throat buzzes as you say it.

::: kana
title: Voiced sounds
が ga | ぎ gi | ぐ gu | げ ge | ご go
ざ za | じ ji | ず zu | ぜ ze | ぞ zo
だ da | ぢ ji | づ zu | で de | ど do
ば ba | び bi | ぶ bu | べ be | ぼ bo
:::

The h row is special. Dashes make it a *b*, and a small circle, called *handakuten*, makes it a *p*.

::: kana
title: The p row
ぱ pa | ぴ pi | ぷ pu | ぺ pe | ぽ po
:::

ぢ and づ sound the same as じ and ず, and they are rare. When you hear *ji* or *zu*, it is almost always じ or ず.

## A small ya, yu or yo

Write や, ゆ or よ at half size after a character from the i column, and the two blend into one beat.

::: example
ja: き + ゃ = きゃ
en: ki + small ya = kya (one beat, not two)
:::

::: vocab
- きょう | today | noun
- しゃしん | photograph | noun
- びょういん | hospital | noun
- ちゃ | tea | noun
:::

Size matters here. びょういん (*byouin*, hospital) and びよういん (*biyouin*, hair salon) differ only in a small よ. Read carefully.

## A small tsu: the pause

A small っ does not make a sound of its own. It doubles the consonant that follows, which you hear as a tiny pause, like the gap in English "hot tea".

::: vocab
- きって | stamp | noun
- がっこう | school | noun
- ちょっと | a little | adverb
:::

Compare きて (*kite*, come) and きって (*kitte*, stamp). Clap the beats: ki-te is two, ki-t-te is three.

## Long vowels

Some vowels are held for two beats. In hiragana you write the extra beat with a vowel character.

- Long *a*, *i* and *u* add あ, い and う: おかあさん (mother), おにいさん (older brother), くうき (air).
- Long *e* is usually written with い: せんせい (teacher) is said *sensee*.
- Long *o* is usually written with う: がっこう (school) is said *gakkoo*.

::: callout
title: Length changes meaning
おばさん is aunt; おばあさん, with a longer *a*, is grandmother. Japanese listeners hear the difference instantly, so it is worth holding the long vowels fully.
:::
