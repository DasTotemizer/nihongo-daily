Japanese verbs are wonderfully regular. They do not change for "I", "you" or "they", and there are only two exceptions worth worrying about. What you do need to know is which **group** a verb belongs to, because each group changes its ending in its own way.

## A verb can be a whole sentence

Just like a noun with だ, a verb on its own is a complete sentence. Every dictionary form ends in an *u* sound: *-u*, *-ku*, *-su*, *-ru* and so on.

::: example
ja: {食|た}べる。
en: (I) eat. / (I) will eat.
note: The dictionary form covers both present and future.
:::

::: example
ja: ジョン が {来|く}る。
en: John is coming. (It's John who will come.)
:::

## The two groups

- **る-verbs** end in -iru or -eru. You change them by dropping る. {食|た}べる (eat) and {見|み}る (see) are る-verbs.
- **う-verbs** are everything else. You change them by changing their last sound. {飲|の}む (drink), {話|はな}す (speak) and {行|い}く (go) are う-verbs.

::: vocab
- {食|た}べる | eat | る-verb
- {見|み}る | see, watch | る-verb
- {寝|ね}る | sleep | る-verb
- {飲|の}む | drink | う-verb
- {話|はな}す | speak | う-verb
- {待|ま}つ | wait | う-verb
- {買|か}う | buy | う-verb
:::

## The catch: some -iru and -eru verbs are う-verbs

A few common verbs end in -iru or -eru but belong to the う group anyway. There is no way to tell from the dictionary form, so they are worth learning as a small set.

::: vocab
- {帰|かえ}る | go home | う-verb
- {入|はい}る | enter | う-verb
- {知|し}る | know | う-verb
- {走|はし}る | run | う-verb
- {切|き}る | cut | う-verb
:::

If a verb does *not* end in -iru or -eru, it is always a う-verb. That rule has no exceptions.

## The two irregular verbs

::: vocab
- する | do | irregular
- {来|く}る | come | irregular
:::

する is everywhere, because Japanese turns many nouns into verbs with it: {勉強|べんきょう}する (study), テニスをする (play tennis).

::: callout
title: Why the groups matter
In the next lesson, {食|た}べる becomes {食|た}べない (don't eat) and {飲|の}む becomes {飲|の}まない (don't drink). Same meaning, different method, because they are in different groups.
:::
