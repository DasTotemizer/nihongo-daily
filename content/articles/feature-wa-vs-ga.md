は and が both seem to mark the subject, so learners often swap them. They do different jobs. Here are two questions that settle most cases.

## Question one: is it new information?

は points at something you are already talking about, and says something about it. The interesting part comes **after** は.

が points at something new. The interesting part is the word **before** が.

::: dialogue
A: {誰|だれ} が {来|き}た？ // Who came?
B: {田中|たなか}さん が {来|き}た。 // Tanaka came.
:::

The question asks *who*, so the answer, {田中|たなか}さん, is the new information. It takes が.

::: dialogue
A: {田中|たなか}さん は？ // What about Tanaka?
B: {田中|たなか}さん は {来|き}た よ。 // Tanaka came, yes.
:::

Now {田中|たなか}さん is the topic. The news is that they came, so は fits.

## Question two: is it a question word?

Question words like {誰|だれ} (who) and {何|なに} (what) are always new information. They never take は as a subject.

::: mistake
wrong: {誰|だれ} は {来|き}た？
right: {誰|だれ} が {来|き}た？
why: Question words are unknown by nature, so they take が.
:::

## The contrast trick

は can also contrast two things: this one, as opposed to that one.

::: example
ja: {肉|にく} は {食|た}べる が、{魚|さかな} は {食|た}べない。
en: Meat, I eat. Fish, I don't.
note: The が in the middle here means "but". It is a different が, which joins two sentences.
:::

## Set phrases with が

Some words like {好|す}き, ある and いる usually mark their thing with が.

::: example
ja: {猫|ねこ} が {好|す}き だ。
en: (I) like cats.
:::

::: callout
title: Don't panic
Using the wrong one rarely makes you misunderstood. Native speakers will still follow you. Keep reading, and the feel for it will come.
:::
