Kanji are the characters Japanese borrowed from Chinese, starting around 1,500 years ago. Each one carries a meaning, and most also carry a sound. A newspaper uses around 2,000 of them regularly. That number scares people, so let's put it in proportion.

You do not need 2,000 kanji to read this paper. Every kanji here comes with furigana, the small readings printed above it, until you choose to turn them off. You will meet kanji one word at a time, in sentences you understand.

## Pictures, then ideas

Some of the simplest kanji began as drawings.

::: vocab
- {山|やま} | mountain | noun
- {川|かわ} | river | noun
- {日|ひ} | sun, day | noun
- {木|き} | tree | noun
- {人|ひと} | person | noun
:::

Most kanji are combinations. {休|やす}む (to rest) puts {人|ひと} (person) beside {木|き} (tree): a person leaning against a tree. Not every kanji has a story that neat, but many have a part that hints at meaning and another that hints at sound.

## Why keep them?

Japanese has a lot of words that sound the same. Written in kana, they look the same too. Kanji tell them apart at a glance.

::: example
ja: {橋|はし} ・ {箸|はし}
en: bridge, chopsticks: both read hashi
note: In speech, pitch and context separate them. In writing, kanji do.
:::

Kanji also make text faster to read. Japanese does not put spaces between words, so the switch between kanji and hiragana shows where words begin and end.

## One kanji, several readings

This is the part that surprises learners. Most kanji have more than one reading.

- **Kun readings** are native Japanese words attached to the kanji. {山|やま} (yama) is a kun reading.
- **On readings** come from the old Chinese pronunciation, and usually appear in compound words. {山|さん} is the on reading, as in {富士山|ふじさん} (Mount Fuji).

::: example
ja: {日本|にほん}
en: Japan: two kanji, sun and origin, read nihon
:::

::: example
ja: {日曜日|にちようび}
en: Sunday: the same {日|ひ} kanji, read two different ways in one word
:::

You do not need to memorise readings as lists. Learn words, and the readings come with them. That is how Japanese children learn too.

::: callout
title: Kana that come after kanji
In {食|た}べる (to eat), only the first part is kanji. The hiragana after it, called *okurigana*, carry the grammar and change as the verb changes: {食|た}べた (ate), {食|た}べない (don't eat).
:::
