Open any Japanese newspaper and you will see three kinds of characters side by side. Rounded, flowing ones. Sharp, angular ones. And dense, complex ones that look like small pictures. These are **hiragana**, **katakana** and **kanji**, and a normal Japanese sentence uses all three.

That is less chaotic than it sounds. Each script has its own job, and once you know the jobs, a sentence starts to sort itself out in front of you.

## The three jobs

::: example
ja: {私|わたし} は コーヒー が {好|す}き です。
en: I like coffee.
note: {私|わたし} and {好|す} are kanji, コーヒー (coffee) is katakana, and everything else is hiragana.
:::

- **Kanji** carry meaning. Most nouns, and the stems of verbs and adjectives, are written in kanji. {私|わたし} means "I"; {好|す} is the core of "like".
- **Hiragana** carry grammar. Particles, verb endings and small connecting words are written in hiragana. は, が and です are all hiragana.
- **Katakana** mark words from other languages, plus names, sounds and emphasis. コーヒー is the English word *coffee*, borrowed into Japanese.

Hiragana and katakana are both *kana*: each character stands for a sound, not a meaning. They cover exactly the same set of sounds, just in two different styles, a little like capital and lower-case letters.

## Japanese sounds are regular

Before learning a single character, it helps to know how few sounds there are. Japanese has five vowels, and they never change.

::: vocab
- あ | a, as in "father" | vowel
- い | i, as in "machine" | vowel
- う | u, as in "food", but with relaxed lips | vowel
- え | e, as in "bed" | vowel
- お | o, as in "more", without the r | vowel
:::

Almost every other sound is a consonant followed by one of these vowels: ka, ki, ku, ke, ko; sa, shi, su, se, so; and so on. There is one consonant that can stand alone, ん (n). That is nearly the whole system.

## Every beat counts

Japanese is spoken in even beats, each roughly the same length. A word like さくら (cherry blossom) has three beats: sa-ku-ra. Nothing is swallowed, and no syllable is stressed much harder than the others.

Instead of stress, Japanese uses **pitch**: some beats are a little higher, some a little lower. You do not need to study pitch yet. Listening closely and copying what you hear will take you a long way.

::: callout
title: About romaji
Romaji means writing Japanese in Latin letters, like *sakura*. It is handy for your first few days, which is why this paper can show it. It is switched off by default because the sooner you read kana, the faster everything else becomes.
:::
