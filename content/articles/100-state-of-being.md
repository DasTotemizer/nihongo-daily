In English, a sentence needs a subject and a verb: "It is a cat." Japanese is far more relaxed. A single noun can be a complete sentence, and the context fills in the rest.

::: example
ja: ねこ。
en: (It's) a cat.
:::

## Declaring with だ

Add だ (da) after a noun and you turn it into a firm statement: something *is* that thing.

::: breakdown
ja: {学生|がくせい} だ。
en: (I'm / he's / she's) a student.
- {学生|がくせい} = student
- だ = is: declares that this is the case
:::

There is no word for "I", "he" or "she" in that sentence. Japanese leaves out whatever is obvious. If someone asks what you do and you answer {学生|がくせい}だ, it is clear you mean yourself.

::: example
ja: {先生|せんせい} だ。
en: (She's) a teacher.
:::

::: example
ja: {元気|げんき} だ。
en: (I'm) well.
note: {元気|げんき} means healthy or full of energy. With だ it becomes a statement about how someone is.
:::

## When to leave だ out

だ is casual and fairly blunt. In everyday conversation people often drop it, especially when answering a question, because the noun alone already sounds complete.

::: dialogue
Ken: {明日|あした}は？ // And tomorrow?
Yui: {休|やす}み。 // A day off.
:::

Saying {休|やす}みだ would be fine too, but a touch more assertive.

::: callout
title: Casual first, polite later
This paper follows Tae Kim's order: casual Japanese first, then polite forms. The polite version of だ is です, which you will meet in Stage 3. Learning the plain forms first makes the polite ones easy, because they are built on top.
:::

## だ does not ask questions

You cannot use だ to ask a question. {学生|がくせい}だ？ with a rising voice sounds odd. In casual speech, the noun alone with a rising tone does the job.

::: example
ja: {学生|がくせい}？
en: (Are you) a student?
:::
